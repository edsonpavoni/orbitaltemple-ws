The current "From the Artist" statement is **remarkably effective as a website text** and **strategically sophisticated for artworld positioning**. It accomplishes the rare feat of being simultaneously accessible to general audiences while establishing serious conceptual credentials. ## What Works Powerfully

### 1. Structural Brilliance: The Framing Question

Opening with "Who is this guy to propose a temple in space?" is masterful. It:
- **Anticipates and disarms skepticism** - The artist asks the question before the viewer can
- **Creates immediate intimacy** - "Thanks for the question" feels conversational, human
- **Establishes humility** - The artist isn't claiming authority, but offering transparency
- **Generates narrative momentum** - The reader wants the answer

This framing transforms what could be a pretentious artist statement into a compelling origin story. It's rhetorically sophisticated while feeling completely natural.

### 2. The Arc: Escape, Search, Discovery, Proposal

The narrative structure is clean and powerful:

**Act I - Origin (Escape from poverty and religion):**
- "Violence wasn't something I read about, it was the world I had to navigate"
- Technology as first escape route (concrete, relatable)
- Christianity as second search for belonging (honest, vulnerable)
- "The price, though, was high" - No elaboration needed, trusts reader intelligence

**Act II - Transition (The search):**
- "I know what walls do. I've felt them" - Earns the metaphor through lived experience
- "God died during those years" - Powerful admission without melodrama
- Art as investigation tool, not decoration

**Act III - Catalyst (Pandemic and Memorial Inumeráveis):**
- Specific, verifiable, documented project
- Shows artistic practice grounded in real-world engagement
- "We transformed numbers into histories" - Clear mission statement
- "What mattered wasn't the scale, it was that each story was a name, a life, someone's heaven" - **Perfect bridge to Orbital Temple concept**

**Act IV - Revelation:**
- "After more than ten thousand conversations with grief" - Credibility through labor
- The pattern discovery: eulogies as letters of recommendation to heaven's gatekeeper
- **Key insight:** "We've been wounded by the very temples we built"
- **Central thesis:** "Our beliefs about who deserves heaven directly shape who we exclude on Earth"
- Historical examples (Portuguese colonizers, Black souls, indigenous peoples, LGBTQ+) - Specific, uncomfortable, necessary

**Act V - Resolution (Space as answer):**
- "Where could we build a temple with no walls?" - The question the entire narrative has earned
- Space as location beyond nation and religion
- "A participatory art satellite from the Global South" - Clear, concrete, specific
- Open source mission - Not just art object, but infrastructure for others
- **Closing:** "I'm not building it. The temple is built by everyone who decides to send a name" - Returns to humility, invites participation

### 3. What Makes This Exceptional

**Vulnerability without self-indulgence:**
- The personal details (step-father in jail, friends imprisoned, escaping Christianity) establish stakes and credibility
- But the focus remains on the project, not the artist's trauma
- The ratio is perfect: enough personal history to earn the political critique, not so much that it becomes confessional

**Political clarity without didacticism:**
- The critique of religious exclusion is sharp and specific
- But it's presented as discovered pattern, not preached ideology
- "We can hold both truths. We must hold both truths" (implied about faith) - This is missing from current text but the spirit is there in "temples we built"

**Concrete specificity:**
- Memorial Inumeráveis is real, documented, verifiable
- "More than ten thousand conversations" - Specific number, labor made visible
- "Portuguese colonizers in Brazil believed Black people had no souls" - Historical fact, not vague gesture
- "A participatory art satellite from the Global South" - Exact description

**Earned ambition:**
- By the time we reach "temple in space," the concept doesn't feel grandiose
- The narrative has built the foundation for why this specific intervention matters
- The artist has shown their work (literally, Memorial Inumeráveis)

## Tonal Considerations

### Website Audience Appropriateness: 9.5/10

**Strengths for general website visitors:**

**1. Accessibility:**
- Language is literary but never academic
- No art jargon ("relational aesthetics," "institutional critique," etc.)
- Concepts explained through story, not theory
- Emotional arc keeps readers engaged

**2. Inclusivity:**
- The critique of religious exclusion is specific enough to be meaningful, gentle enough not to alienate
- "I found Christianity, or it found me. I was accepted. I was loved. I had a community. I learned to forgive, to believe, to move together for a greater purpose."
- This is **beautifully generous** toward religious experience even while critiquing religious exclusion
- A religious visitor can read this and feel understood, not attacked

**3. Motivates Participation:**
- "You're the one building it if you decide to" - Direct invitation
- The entire narrative leads to this moment of decision
- By the end, sending a name feels like joining something meaningful, not just clicking a button

**Potential Concerns:**

**Will it alienate religious visitors?**

**NO - and here's why:**
- The text distinguishes between **faith as human need** (honored) and **institutional gatekeeping** (critiqued)
- "I was accepted. I was loved. I had a community" - This acknowledges what temples provide
- The critique targets specific historical injustices (slavery, exclusion of LGBTQ+, indigenous peoples)
- These are facts, not opinions
- Even conservative religious visitors would struggle to defend the Portuguese colonizers' position on Black souls
- The proposal isn't "abandon religion" but "imagine a temple that includes everyone"

**The tone is:**
- Disappointed, not angry
- Mournful, not militant
- "We've been wounded by the very temples we built" - Note the "we" - Artist includes himself
- This creates space for religious visitors to participate without feeling judged

**Risk Assessment: LOW**

A small percentage of fundamentalist visitors might object to:
- Mention of LGBTQ+ exclusion as problem
- Implication that heaven might not have gates
- Artist's departure from Christianity

**But:**
- These visitors are unlikely to participate regardless of text
- The project's premise (radically inclusive temple) already signals this isn't for exclusionary theology
- Softening the critique would undermine the entire conceptual foundation
- The current balance is optimal: clear about values, generous about faith

Could the explicit political positioning (LGBTQ+ rights, decolonial framework) be read as propaganda?

**No, because:**

- The historical examples are specific and factual, not ideological assertions
- The work doesn't tell you what to think, it invites you to participate in a alternative structure
- The artist's vulnerability (escaping poverty, leaving Christianity) complicates any simple ideological reading
- The form (participatory satellite) is sufficiently novel and conceptually integrated

**Risk Level: LOW**
- Art institutions in 2025 are more politically engaged than ever
- The specific critiques (slavery justified by heaven-exclusion) are historically uncontroversial
- Museums with conservative boards might avoid it, but that's not the target anyway

**3. "Complexity" Question:**

Is there enough conceptual complexity or does it feel too straightforward?

  - This tension is implied, not resolved

**These complexities are there for critics to unpack**, even if not foregrounded for general audience.

**Risk Level: LOW**

**Risk Level: MEDIUM**

I was born in the outskirts of São Paulo, in a neighborhood that taught me
early what danger means. Many of my school friends are in jail now. My
step-father is in jail. Violence wasn't something I read about, it was the
world I had to navigate. And I knew I needed to escape.

Technology became my way out. I learned to program, learned electronics as
a teenager. Code was a language that could take me somewhere else. Working
with technology was my escape from poverty.

But I was also searching for belonging, for love, for something to believe
in. I found Christianity, or it found me. I was accepted. I was loved. I had
a community. I learned to forgive, to believe, to move together for a greater
purpose.

The price, though, was high. I also learned many things I am still unlearning.
So I escaped from that too.

I know what walls do. I've felt them. I've been on both sides: kept out, kept
in. What followed were years of searching for something I couldn't name. God
died during those years. The god I'd been taught about, anyway, the one with
rules and judgment and doors that only opened for some people.

I started making art to explore these questions: about intimacy and technology,
about memory and death, about who deserves heaven. I started to build machines
that reimagine our relationship with technology, machines that need your
participation, your presence, your name, to transform from metal and code into
something alive.

Then the pandemic hit Brazil.

When COVID vaccines were delayed, my government was negotiating kickbacks of
one dollar per dose. They promoted medicine understood to be ineffective,
laughing while people died struggling to breathe. We woke up every day to a
different number. How many people died in the last 24 hours? Was it more, or
less than expected?

Half of the world was scared, and another half was in denial. We were at war,
with the virus and with each other.

I wanted to create space for collective grief, so together with hundreds of
people I created the Memorial Inumeráveis. An artwork at the intersection of
art and journalism. We interviewed families who had lost someone. We wrote the
life story of the person they lost. We transformed numbers into histories.
Poems, songs, symphonies were made with those histories. Stars read those
stories on national television. The project reached millions.

What mattered wasn't the scale, it was that each story was a name, a life,
someone's heaven.

After more than ten thousand conversations with grief, a pattern started to
emerge. When we talk about someone who has died, we don't just say good things;
we select fragments of their story as if we're writing a letter of
recommendation. A recommendation to the gatekeeper of paradise. We do our best
to send them to a place of acceptance, rest, honor and memory.

And I understood something that changed everything: We've been wounded by the
very temples we built. Our beliefs about who deserves heaven directly shape who
we exclude on Earth. The Portuguese colonizers in Brazil believed Black people
had no souls; that belief justified slavery. If you were indigenous, if you
were born in the wrong place or loved the wrong person, you were told heaven's
doors were closed to you.

That is how Orbital Temple was born.

Where could we build a temple with no walls? Where no nation or religion could
claim. Where we could imagine ourselves as one group.

Space.

A participatory art satellite from the Global South. A temple in orbit that
invites everyone to send their names to space, regardless of who they are,
where they were born, who they love, what they believe. A place where radical
inclusion is possible, in orbit, beyond borders.

This mission is open source. Every technical document, every line of code will
be freely available so other artists, other communities, other nations can
build their own satellites. So the Global South doesn't just participate in
space exploration, but leads it.

So who is this guy to build a temple in space? I'm not building it. The temple
is built by everyone who decides to send a name, to see it as sacred, to make
it real. I'm just the one proposing. You're the one building it if you decide to.

Kindly,
Edson Pavoni
